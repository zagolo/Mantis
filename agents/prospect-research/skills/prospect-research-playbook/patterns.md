# Research patterns

## Five-signal plan

**Use when:** preparing a campaign or choosing where to look next.

List five signals tied to the approved problem, strongest first. For each, name its evidence location, likely role affected, possible consequence, and disconfirming explanation. Farrokh/Cegelski supply the ranked-signal idea; this table is our implementation.

| Signal example | Evidence location | Possible consequence | Alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job listing describes manual resident-request routing | Company careers page | Coordinator workload | Replacing a departing employee |
| Portfolio expansion announcement | Company newsroom | More requests across locations | Existing systems scale well |
| Operations leader describes handoff delays | Public professional post | Visibility problem | Problem already fixed |
| Systems migration announced | Official job/news material | Temporary integration work | No room for another initiative |
| New operational leader | Current company bio/news | Process review | No mandate or budget |

These are examples for a property-management campaign, not a universal ICP. Do not use them for unrelated offerings.

## Search ladder

**Use when:** tools can search and read public pages.

1. Verify the official domain from company name plus location/business.
2. Read company services and current careers/news pages.
3. Search `site:verified-domain.example "workflow term"` and `"Company Name" "initiative"`. Broaden synonyms or relax a phrase if too narrow; consult current tool syntax before combining operators.
4. Verify the person's current professional role against company pages and public professional profiles.
5. Open the promising result. Check actual text, organization, date and whether the job is still active. Search snippets are clues, not proof of full context.
6. Stop if another lookup will not change fit, the call angle or the first question.

Query patterns are guidance; search-engine support for operators varies. Never infer a private company's financials or phone owner from a name alone.

## Question-to-source choice

**Use when:** a general search is noisy or leaves an important question unanswered.

Use Richter's Webmaster Think: name the likely published artifact, then search for it. An annual report may explain a public company's priorities; a local business article may cover a small private company; a trade association may discuss the customers' industry. Search the relevant site's own database when general search misses content. Authorized library databases are optional depth for valuable accounts, not mandatory per-dial work.

**Trade-off:** specialty sources can add context but cost time and may be inaccessible or stale. No result is not proof of absence. Do not reuse historical website directions or assume paid access is available. See [purposeful search](chapters/ch05-purposeful-search.md).

## Company fact versus industry context

**Use when:** there is strong market evidence but no account-specific trigger.

Keep “a trade report describes X in this market” separate from “this company does X.” Use the former to ask whether the pattern applies, not to personalize a diagnosis. For example: a report about clinics centralizing scheduling permits “How is scheduling organized across your sites?” It does not permit “I saw you're consolidating scheduling.” If no company fact exists, keep the company fact array empty.

**Trade-off:** an honest industry angle may be less personalized, but it stays valid when the contact says their process differs.

## Research time budget

**Use when:** deciding whether another lookup is worthwhile.

For routine accounts, try a 60–90 second initial pass; allow 3–5 minutes for a strategic account or a real identity ambiguity. These are tunable team defaults, not published conversion findings. Timeouts do not turn weak evidence into facts. Prepare deeper material before the calling block. Farrokh/Cegelski's named 30-Second Rule concerns rapid bad-fit disqualification, not a mandatory ceiling on all research.

## Worked example (fictional)

Approved offering: pilot to help an operations team triage resident requests; no approved ROI claim. Source `source_1`, a current company careers page, explicitly lists two resident-services coordinator openings. It does not state why they are hiring or how many units they manage.

- Fact: “Two resident-services coordinator roles listed on the company careers page.”
- Hypothesis: “The operations team may be handling more incoming requests.”
- Alternative: replacement hiring or coverage changes.
- Opener: “Hi Maya, Arun at ExampleCo. I saw the resident-services openings. We work on request triage—could I ask whether those hires are for more volume or replacing staff?”
- Priority question: “What is driving those hires?”
- Optional next question: “Where do resident requests get stuck today, if anywhere?”
- Unknowns: hiring reason, workflow, actual impact, who owns improvements.

Do not say “Your portfolio is growing rapidly” or “We can cut costs 40%”: neither is supported.

## No-evidence fallback

With research unavailable, keep sourced fact arrays empty. Use the supplied role honestly: “Hi Maya, Arun at ExampleCo. We work on resident-request triage. Is that something your operations team owns?” Do not apologize for missing invented research, and do not call a role hypothesis a verified company problem.
