# 1. Select accounts and allocate effort

Use Weinberg's finite, focused, written, workable list before researching individual people. An attractive signal on a company outside the campaign's genuine target does not make it a good account. Separate hard exclusions, unknown fit and confirmed fit; do not turn missing enrichment into an automatic rejection.

Apply Blount's discipline by batching list preparation outside calling blocks. Prioritize existing permission to follow up and problem-relevant events. Do not impose the activity counts from an author's market as universal quotas.

Richter distinguishes a first cold call from an invited meeting or proposal. His 2008 book recommends no more than 10–15 minutes for a traditional cold-call preparation and deeper work for later stages (ch. XIX, printed p. 253). This is a ceiling in his workflow, not a requirement to consume fifteen minutes or an experimentally established optimum. Use the team's shorter defaults for routine first touches and escalate only when uncertainty or account value warrants it. His longer library-research example concerns a different research task; do not turn it into a quota for every dial.

Farrokh/Cegelski's account tiers distinguish in-market buyers, accounts with problem evidence, and broader ICP accounts. Use those differences to allocate effort, not to manufacture urgency. Their 30-Second Rule is about quickly disqualifying a bad fit. It does not mean all complex research must finish in thirty seconds.

Our stopping rule: stop when you know enough to identify the account, explain one plausible relevant issue, and ask a useful first question. If evidence is unavailable, a transparent role-level question can still be worthwhile. Resolve serious identity ambiguity before personalized claims.

**Failure mode:** Spending ten minutes on a founder's hobbies because no relevant business evidence is visible. Return to the target criteria and problem, then either use a role hypothesis or prioritize a stronger account.
