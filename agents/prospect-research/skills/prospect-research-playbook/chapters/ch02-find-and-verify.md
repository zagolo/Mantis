# 2. Find and verify the useful signal

Combine Sobczak's factual and situational intelligence with Richter's Fourth R. Factual intelligence says who the organization is and what it does. Situational intelligence concerns developments and circumstances that might make a conversation relevant. Do not substitute personal trivia for either.

Rank five signals that connect to the campaign's actual offering. Read the strongest accessible source first. Prefer current job descriptions, official operational announcements, and first-person professional discussion of the problem. An aggregator can identify a lead; verify its important claims when possible.

Record the entity, source, event date, exact supported proposition, and uncertainty. A company's claim about itself remains a company claim. A dated article about an old expansion is not evidence of expansion this month. A role title is not proof of spending authority.

## Four checks before using a signal

Richter repeatedly emphasizes relevant, timely, credible and objective information (ch. III–V). Apply these as questions rather than treating any source category as automatically trustworthy:

| Check | Research decision |
|---|---|
| Relevant | Could this fact change fit, a useful question, or the next step for the campaign? Otherwise leave it out. |
| Timely | Does the event date match the claim being made? An archive or old biography can explain history but cannot establish a current role or initiative. |
| Credible | Is the publisher identifiable, and does the actual source support the exact claim? An aggregator listing is a lead for verification. |
| Objective | What interest or limitation shapes the source? Preserve a company's self-description or a vendor survey as attributed claims, not neutral proof. |

Check company, industry and professional-person context separately. A trade report can explain a sector pressure without proving the target experiences it. A customer-industry shift may be relevant to the prospect's revenue, but that link remains a hypothesis until confirmed. If no company-specific trigger exists, an industry-informed question is legitimate; do not rewrite the trend as a company fact.

Richter's ch. XIII suggests job listings, local business news and patents as discovery routes. Check live status and identity: a job advert does not prove expansion or a broken workflow; a company mentioned in a patent may not own it, and ownership does not prove commercialization. Use historical website snapshots only as historical evidence.

Search absence can reflect coverage, access, language or a poor query. For a valuable unresolved issue, search the relevant trade publication, association, company portal or authorized library database directly. Record what was actually accessible. An inaccessible report is not evidence of its contents. [Purposeful search](ch05-purposeful-search.md) gives a bounded sequence; this is optional research depth, not a prerequisite for every call.

Richter's later public interview also offers a “three by five” preparation heuristic. It remains separate public material; do not attribute it to the supplied 2008 edition.

**Failure mode:** A query returns two companies with the same name. Verify domain, location and business before taking facts from either. If unresolved, keep the conflict in unknowns and omit a personalized trigger.

**Connection:** [Evidence to conversation](ch03-evidence-to-conversation.md) separates what happened from what it might mean.
