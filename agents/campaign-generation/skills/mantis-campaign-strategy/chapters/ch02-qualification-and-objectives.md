# Questions, criteria and objectives

Questions help the caller learn; criteria tell the engine what a confirmed answer means. Keep those distinct. A question may ask how work happens; a criterion may ask whether the contact's organization owns that workflow. Neither asserts that the prospect has a problem.

Use the smallest set of required criteria that makes the campaign outcome meaningful. If discovery is the objective, requiring a purchase-ready budget or final decision-maker on the first call may reject useful conversations. If a capability is genuinely limited to a business type or workflow, a confirmed mismatch can justify disqualification.

Every `onNo` needs a defensible interpretation:

| Confirmed answer | Possible mapping | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The organization does not own the workflow the offering addresses | disqualified | Structural mismatch with this offering |
| The right owner wants to revisit after a dated project | defer | Timing can change |
| The person cannot describe the operational problem | unknown | Their knowledge does not establish the company's situation |

These are examples, not default criteria for every campaign. Do not treat an absent answer as a confirmed no.

Sobczak defines the primary objective as what the other person will do; a secondary objective gives the caller another appropriate outcome. In this engine, both require truthful labels and respect for willingness. A request for information is meaningful when real, but is not a booked meeting. A research conversation should not be scored against sales-close outcomes.

Keenan's supplied *Gap Selling* problem/impact/cause and four question types organize questions. Probing seeks a concrete fact; process seeks the workflow; provoking tests a possible consequence; validating checks interpretation. His critique of early BANT gates does not erase affordability or actual buying-process requirements before a purchase. It cannot supply actual impact numbers, root causes or urgency for an account. Full discovery belongs in a receptive conversation, not in a compulsory interrogation before every cold-call meeting.
