# Discovery and qualification

## Core idea

Discovery is useful after the buyer is willing to talk. Understand their actual workflow and desired change. A public signal is not an admission, and a cold-call interruption is not permission for a full diagnostic interview.

## Prepare a map, then follow the answer

Sobczak's **Possible Value Proposition** stays provisional until the person confirms value. His **Smart Questions** connect possible benefits to actual circumstances. Keenan's **Problem Identification Chart (PIC)** prepares three columns: **problem, impact, root cause**. Before contact, these describe possible issues the approved offering can address; they are not findings about this buyer.

Keenan's **current state** has five elements: literal/physical facts, problems, impact, root causes and emotional state. Use this map to notice missing information. A technical difficulty explains how a workflow fails; a business consequence explains why that matters. They are different layers, and neither proves the proposed product will fix the cause. Record emotion only when the prospect actually expresses it; a text fragment is not evidence of fear, frustration or urgency.

Choose the next question by the missing information:

| Question type | Purpose | Original example |
|---|---|---|
| **Probing** | Obtain a concrete fact, quantity, definition or exception | “What does ‘too slow’ mean in a typical week?” |
| **Process** | Learn how work actually moves, including handoffs | “Walk me through how the request reaches the coordinator.” |
| **Provoking** | Gently test a consequence or alternative without presupposing it | “What happens if that handoff waits until the next morning?” |
| **Validating** | Check interpretation, usually with a closed confirmation | “So the delay is in assignment, rather than entry—is that right?” |

Do not ask all four in order. Skip answered information, follow a meaningful answer, and summarize before changing topics. Open-ended questions are useful; vague answers still need clarification when the missing detail affects the decision. If the prospect only has time for a short exchange, establish one relevant issue and propose a later conversation if wanted.

## Future state and the gap

Ask what outcome would be worthwhile and what is driving the change. Their desired **future state** is not the seller's target. **Future State − Current State = The Gap** compares like quantities with units and periods. Report percentage-point changes accurately: moving from a 30% rate to 35% is five percentage points, not automatically 5% relative growth.

The gap's size is not the offered service's causal impact, recoverable saving or guaranteed ROI. Distinguish a desired outcome from an achievable outcome. Compare the benefit with implementation work, cost, change risk and competing priorities. A small gap or a cause the offer cannot solve can justify stopping. Do not widen the gap by inventing consequences or converting estimates into facts.

Keenan criticizes treating BANT as an early gate; he does not eliminate qualification. A missing purchase budget or a non-signatory contact need not disqualify exploratory discovery. Actual affordability, capability fit and buying process still matter before a commercial commitment. Follow the campaign's configured criteria, not a generic author's ideal buyer.

## Original worked example

A property-operations contact says: “A coordinator re-enters each maintenance request, but it only takes a few minutes.” Do not jump to salary savings. A probing question is: “Roughly how many requests need that second entry in a typical week?”

If the answer is ten, the gap may be small. If it is three hundred with urgent requests missed, follow the consequence: “What happens when an urgent one is missed?” That answer may reveal a routing issue rather than a data-entry issue. A process question can locate the handoff; a validating question checks that the cause is understood. Volume alone establishes neither urgency nor technical fit.

If the buyer later confirms two minutes per request and three hundred per week, the stated workload is ten hours per week. That arithmetic does not promise ten hours saved. Exceptions, human review and setup can limit recovery. If the buyer's priority is faster urgent routing, an agreed pilot measure might concern routing time rather than labor reduction; the threshold and scope must come from them and the approved offering.

## Qualification contract

Use only campaign criterion IDs. A `yes` or `no` requires the contact's explicit relevant confirmation or denial, with a short exact evidence quotation. Research is not qualification evidence. Seller assertions are not contact statements. A job title is not purchase authority. A generic “yes” after multiple questions does not confirm every part; clarify what matters.

Keep missing evidence unknown. Follow the contact's correction over earlier research or inference. A friendly conversation, a large company or a desired future state is not a purchase commitment. Record supported facts distinctly from unresolved hypotheses and seller promises.

## Why it works and where it fails

Selecting a question by the missing information makes the next cue responsive. The method fails when the coach treats its PIC as a diagnosis, pressures a buyer to reveal sensitive detail, or uses a hypothetical impact to prove its own offer. Keenan's **CRM Challenge** is useful in review: can the notes identify this opportunity by its specific context, rather than generic “needs efficiency”? It is a specificity check, not permission to infer missing detail.
