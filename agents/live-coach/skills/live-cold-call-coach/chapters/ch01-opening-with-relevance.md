# Opening with relevance

## Core idea

The opening earns enough attention to discover whether the call is relevant. Use one true reason for this person to listen; do not try to impress them with a dossier or conceal the caller's identity.

## Decision method

1. Check identity, prior relationship and campaign objective. When identity is uncertain, a brief confirmation is better than a confidently wrong name. If the prior call was a colleague's, identify it honestly.
2. Choose one current business fact from supplied research that relates to an approved offer. Preserve its uncertainty and date. A hiring page can establish an open role; it cannot establish budget, workload failure or willingness to buy.
3. Use Farrokh/Cegelski's **Tailored Permission Opener**: relevant context, honest acknowledgment of the interruption, short permission to explain. Include identity naturally and answer it immediately when asked.
4. If permission is given, use a short **Problem Proposition**: **Triggering Problem → One-Sentence Solution → Interest-Based CTA**. Keep Sobczak's **Possible Value Proposition** provisional. Ask whether the situation resonates rather than announcing a diagnosis.
5. Stop and listen. If they are unreceptive, route to the boundary rules instead of completing the script.

Without account-specific evidence, say why the role or business type could be relevant, without pretending a special trigger exists. If the contact corrects the fact, acknowledge it immediately; do not keep using a cached opener.

## Original illustrative example

Assume, for this example only, that the campaign approves a maintenance-request triage service and the company's current careers page verifies two maintenance-coordinator openings. No workload problem has been confirmed.

Possible opener: “Hi Maya, Alex from Example. I saw the maintenance-coordinator openings on your site. Could I take 20 seconds to explain why that prompted my call?”

After permission, the caller can explore whether the hires address increasing request volume or another need. The coach should choose that question based on the next contact turn. It must not claim the company is overwhelmed, losing residents, or spending a particular amount.

If Maya says the hiring page is outdated, the next line acknowledges the correction. The research hypothesis loses priority; the conversation is now the evidence.

This example is an authored application of B1/B2, not book dialogue or an approved claim for the actual campaign.

## Gatekeepers and voicemail

For a gatekeeper, identify the caller and give concise business context. Ask who handles the relevant area or whether there is a preferred route. Sobczak's helper/relationship view and Farrokh/Cegelski's efficient routing differ; use respectful transparency. Do not imply the buyer requested the call, invent a sent email or pretend familiarity.

For voicemail, follow the campaign's selected objective. A callback-oriented message and an email-supporting message are different tactics. Choose one and include only true context. Do not let a live-conversation opener become a long voicemail. Leaving a message is not a live connection or evidence of interest.

## Failure modes and reconciliation

- **False familiarity:** using “heard our name?” around invented peers creates a factual claim the campaign cannot support.
- **Trivia personalization:** a university or hobby mention does not explain the business reason to call.
- **Stacked openings:** context, long introduction, permission, value story and meeting ask all at once force too many decisions.
- **Universal script rules:** Sobczak, Blount and Farrokh/Cegelski order identity and context differently. Use a coherent short approach, then evaluate by audience; never hide identity because a script says context must come first.
